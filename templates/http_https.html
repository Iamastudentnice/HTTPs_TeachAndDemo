What Does HTTPS Actually Do?

HTTP
Your information travels without encryption. Someone monitoring the connection may be able to read it.
HTTPS
Your information is encrypted while travelling between your browser and the website, making it much harder for others to read.

    Key words in plain English

    Browser - the app you use to visit websites, such as Chrome, Safari or Firefox.

    Server - the computer that provides the website.

    Encryption - scrambling information so unauthorised people cannot easily read it. Only someone with the right key can unscramble it.

    TLS - the technology that protects HTTPS connections. HTTPS is simply HTTP sent through TLS.

    Certificate - information used to verify a website's identity, a bit like an ID card for the website.

    Packet - a small piece of data travelling across the network. Your message is carried in packets.

    Learn more: what HTTPS does and does not protect

    It protects your data in transit. The contents of what you send and receive are encrypted, and any change made on the way is detected.

    It does not hide everything. The network can still see which website you connect to, your IP address, and when and how much data is sent.

    The certificate proves identity, it does not do the encrypting. It lets your browser check it is talking to the real website. The data itself is encrypted with a separate secret key agreed at the start (see Lab 02).

    It cannot judge the website. Scam sites can use HTTPS too, and HTTPS cannot protect a device that already has malware.

    Watching is not the same as tampering. The eavesdropper here only watches (a passive attack). Attackers who actively change or fake traffic are covered in the MITM lab.
